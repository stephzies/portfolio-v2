01 / At a glance

Designing from both sides of the experience

Startzie is a personal planning application I designed to bring tasks, ideas, and daily priorities into a connected, intuitive experience.

The project began with a problem I repeatedly encountered while organizing my own work and personal life. As both the designer and primary user, I could identify friction through everyday use and quickly iterate on solutions with AI-assisted development.

This case study follows how I translated those observations into design decisions, then continued refining the experience as new problems emerged.

Role: Independent Product Designer
Responsibilities: Product strategy, discovery, information architecture, UX/UI, and iterative evaluation
Development: AI-assisted implementation
Status: Working product, ongoing development

02 / The challenge

When organizing became another task

I started using Chrome bookmarks for quick access to work tools, bills, and important links. As my collection grew, the bookmark bar became a long dropdown list, so I began organizing links into groups like Money and Design.

Eventually, those groups became difficult to navigate too. I created smaller folders within them, separating things like design tools, tutorials, portfolio resources, and inspiration.

Everything was becoming more organized, but finding what I needed still meant digging through lists and folders. It reminded me of storing something important in a box at the back of a drawer.

I encountered similar friction while experimenting with Start.me dashboards and Notion workspaces. I kept spending time organizing and searching for information when I wanted to spend that time actually using it.

03 / Discovery

From groups to connections

As I kept reorganizing my bookmarks, I started questioning the structure itself.

I had links and resources grouped by topic, but I wanted to understand how those groups related to one another and how the resources within them supported the work I was doing.

I began thinking about a dashboard where I could see those relationships at a glance and quickly find what I needed to move forward with my daily tasks.

Key insight

Grouping resources wasn't the same as understanding how they supported the work I was doing.

04 / Define the direction

From the big picture to focused action

That insight shaped the direction for Startzie. I began designing Categories as contextual dashboards for different areas of life and work, bringing related projects, tasks, notes, and resources together in one place.

Each Category would provide a bigger-picture view of what was underway and what still needed attention. From there, I wanted to move into today's priorities and individual tasks without losing the context behind them.

The defining question

How might I move from a Category's big-picture context to focused daily action while keeping relevant projects, notes, and tools within reach?

Design Priorities

01 / See the whole picture

Make ongoing work, progress, and next steps scannable within a Category.

02 / Bridge overview and action

Make it clear which tasks advance the bigger project or area of life.

03 / Focus with context

Keep supporting notes, files, and tools accessible when they matter rather than showing everything at once.

These priorities guided the next stage: exploring how an overview, a planning space, and focused work could serve different purposes without asking one screen to do everything.

05 / Explore and structure

Give each view a job

An early version of Startzie brought planning, capture, notes, and broader project context into the same dashboard. It gave me access to many useful elements at once, but the experience felt crowded. Too many activities were competing for attention.

Before separating the product into clearer views, I explored ways to reduce that overwhelm within the dashboard itself. I introduced collapsed sections to hide secondary details, stronger color contrast to establish visual hierarchy, and tabs at the top to divide the experience into more manageable modes.

I also moved Categories out of the main Overview cards and into the sidebar. This made them accessible from any view while freeing up space for daily priorities.

These refinements made the information easier to scan, but they also revealed a bigger issue: the problem wasn't only visual density. Different kinds of work needed different spaces.

Different views, different purposes

  1. 01

    Today / Overview: Get oriented. See what needs attention now and what is coming up.

  2. 02

    Plan: Make decisions. Organize, schedule, and rearrange work.

  3. 03

    Categories: Understand the bigger picture. Understand ongoing commitments, projects, ideas, and next steps in an area of life or work.

When working on an individual task, Current Focus keeps the task and supporting context within reach.

The Tradeoff

Separating these responsibilities introduced more navigation, but reduced how much information competed for attention at once. The goal wasn't to hide information. It was to show the right level of detail for the job each view needed to do.

That distinction became especially important as I began using the product and discovered where seemingly consistent interactions didn't actually serve the purpose of a particular view.

06 / Design decisions

What using the product revealed

Using Startzie in my own daily routine exposed friction that wasn't obvious while designing individual features. Each of these interactions worked, but its behavior affected the larger workflow in ways I hadn't fully anticipated.

01 / One routine, not six tasks

What I noticed

Opening my Health Category revealed a stack of nearly identical Daily Workout entries. Each future occurrence appeared as another active task. That level of detail made sense in a date-based plan, but not in a Category meant to show the bigger picture of an area of life.

What I changed

I separated how the same recurring work is represented across views. A Category displays one routine for the recurring commitment; calendar and planning views retain its dated occurrences. The Category inventory groups items by their recurring-series identity, not simply by matching task names.

Why it mattered

The underlying tasks didn't need to change. Their presentation needed to reflect the question that view was answering: What commitments exist here? rather than What happens on each date?

02 / Edit a task without losing my place

What I noticed

Opening a task expanded a long editor directly inside the dashboard. Editing Daily Workout pushed the daily plan and surrounding context down the page, just when I needed to stay oriented.

What I changed

I replaced the inline editor with a centered task-detail dialog. Common actions remain easy to reach; less frequent fields move into deeper Schedule and Details areas. The dashboard remains in place beneath the editor instead of being rearranged around it.

Why it mattered

Editing should be a focused detour, not a disruption to the larger workflow. The dialog introduced a temporary overlay, but avoided displacing the page I intended to return to.

03 / Move tasks more directly

What I noticed

Moving tasks through a menu worked, but felt indirect when I was adjusting my plan. I also discovered that an interaction could support dragging without making it especially easy to grab and reposition a task.

What I changed

I introduced a shared drag-and-drop interaction across task cards and planning areas while keeping the Move menu available as another way to move tasks. I then refined task-card controls and drop behavior, and expanded the draggable area so I could grab the card itself instead of aiming for a small handle.

Why it mattered

Moving a task became a more direct part of planning. Refining where I could grab the card made the interaction easier to use, while the menu still offered an alternative when dragging wasn't practical.

What these iterations taught me

Using Startzie in my daily routine challenged design decisions and assumptions I hadn't questioned during development.

Recurring tasks worked in Planning Views but cluttered Categories. The inline editor allowed me to change a task but disrupted the dashboard. Moving tasks through a dropdown was functional, but it made rearranging my day unnecessarily cumbersome.

Building quickly with AI also revealed a weakness in my process. I often prompted for what I needed in one view without fully considering how the implementation would affect another. Some interaction patterns emerged without me deliberately designing them.

I learned to evaluate the experience as a connected system, looking beyond whether individual features worked to understand how they affected the overall workflow.

I also became more intentional about AI-assisted development. Iterating quickly helped me discover problems through use, but it didn't replace the need to think through interactions, anticipate dependencies, and critically evaluate what was being built.

07 / Outcome and reflection

From organizing information to supporting action

I began this project trying to make it easier to find the things I needed. My bookmarks, folders, and dashboards had become increasingly organized, but I was still spending too much time figuring out how everything fit together.

Startzie has evolved into a working application that I continue to use and refine. Categories provide context for different areas of life and work, Overview helps me understand what needs attention today, and Plan gives me space to organize and adjust my tasks.

The most meaningful progress came from establishing clearer relationships between those parts of the experience and refining how they worked together through everyday use.

How this changed my approach to design

Building Startzie challenged more than my original ideas about organization. It also challenged how I approached designing and developing a product.

Working with AI allowed me to experiment and implement ideas quickly, but it also reinforced the importance of anticipating dependencies, questioning implementation choices, and evaluating how individual interactions affect the experience as a whole.

I've become more deliberate about examining my own decisions, recognizing friction, and revisiting assumptions as the product evolves.

What I still need to learn

Startzie has been shaped primarily by my own needs and habits. That gives me valuable firsthand insight, but it also means there are assumptions I haven't tested with other people.

My next priority is external usability testing. I want to understand whether new users can navigate Categories, move naturally between the bigger picture and daily planning, and find the context they need to make progress.

I'm also interested in exploring two questions:

  • Can Categories scale? As more areas of life and work are added, could navigating them become another organizational burden? This is a potential challenge I haven't encountered yet.
  • Can onboarding create meaningful connections earlier? I'd like to explore helping users identify broader life goals and establish the Categories that support them. This remains a future design direction.

Final reflection

What began with an overcrowded bookmarks bar led me to a broader design question: How can an experience help people understand how their work fits together and make it easier to move forward?

Startzie is still evolving, but that question continues to guide both the product and the way I approach design.
